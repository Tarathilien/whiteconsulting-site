AI governance

Your staff already use AI. Who is steering?

Nearly every nonprofit now uses AI in some form. Far fewer have written down what staff may put into it. That gap is where the damage happens, and it is not hard to close.

What this looks like in practice

A policy on paper was not enough.

On April 29, 2026, an employee of Community First Health Plans uploaded a file of member information to ChatGPT from a home network. The file included names, addresses, dates of birth, member ID numbers and prescription information. The organization says the upload broke its policy and got around its firewall.

The organization had rules and technical safeguards, and the data still left. Afterward it revoked the employee’s access, demanded deletion from OpenAI, notified regulators, offered affected members identity monitoring, and began adding VPN requirements, log-on time limits and retraining. It announced the incident two months later, on June 29.

That is the difference between a template policy and a real assessment. A written rule tells staff what not to do. An assessment finds where data can actually leave, which tools people use outside the office, and what controls and training close those gaps before an incident, not after.

Funders and donors are starting to ask as well. Many funders now ask grant applicants to disclose AI use, and a written policy backed by real controls is the easiest answer to give.

Where to start

Every organization has to start somewhere. Start with a policy.

A written AI policy turns a vague worry into decisions: which tools staff may use, what data stays out, and who reviews what AI produces. Everything else builds on that.

I know it feels like a lot. The rules are new, the tools change constantly, and nobody on your staff was hired to figure this out. But ignoring AI is not realistic. Your staff, your funders and your donors are already dealing with it.

AI is a tool, like email or a spreadsheet. It works best when you decide how it is used, not when you are afraid of it.
